In the Monday section (on February 23rd), we covered up through Regression Splines, the topic of ISLP Section 7.4. Towards the beginning of the lecture, though, I also gave a brief conceptual overview of Smoothing Splines, as an alternative way to “arrive at” splines:

With Regression Splines we start from the explicit assumption that we’re going to “chop” the domain of some feature \(X \in [a,b]\) at \(K\) knot points \(\xi_1 < \xi_2 < \cdots < \xi_{k-1} < \xi_k\), thus splitting \([a,b]\) into \(K + 1\) separately-modeled pieces \([a,\xi_1], [\xi_1, \xi_2], \ldots, [\xi_{k-1},\xi_k], [\xi_k, b]\). This means that, when estimating a Regression Spline model, we “jump” immediately to the task of choosing the number of knot points \(K\) and their locations.

To “arrive at” Smoothing Splines, we take an alternative approach. We do not jump right into the task of choosing knot points — in fact, we don’t assume up front that we’ll be “chopping” the domain up at all. Instead, we start with the much more basic assumption that we want to learn a function \(\hat{f}(x)\) that does a good job of fitting the data (as measured by MSE, for example, in a regression scenario), but that we also want to somehow constrain the “wigglyness” of \(\hat{f}(x)\)1.

How might we quantify the degree of “wigglyness” exhibited by a function \(\widehat{f}\) over \([a,b]\), the domain of \(X\)? At this level of abstraction there may be an aesthetic aspect here, where one person’s intuition may not exactly match with another person’s, but if we more concretely focus on the kinds of “wigglyness” that tend to indicate overfitting, we can think of a degree-\(N\) polynomial fit to \(N\) datapoints as the canonical example:

The massive “swerve” between \(x = 1\) and \(x = 2\) — where although all the true \(y\) values are positive, \(\hat{f}\) predicts values down near -400 as it moves from 0 to 1 — is what we’d like to penalize, and (for the reasons discussed in lecture) we can achieve this by applying a penalty \(\lambda\) to the [continuous] “sum” of squared second derivatives, giving us the following “Error + Complexity Penalty” optimization task, much like Lasso’s:

\[ \hat{f}^* = \argmin_{\hat{f}}\left[ ~\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{f}(x_i))^2}_{\mathclap{\small\text{Mean Squared Error (MSE)}}} + \overbrace{\lambda}^{\mathclap{\text{Penalty}}} \underbrace{\int_{a}^{b} \left[\hat{f}''(x)\right]^2dx}_{\text{Total Wigglyness}} \right]. \tag{1}\]

What makes this approach relevant to our current topic is that, in fact, even though we didn’t start out assuming that we wanted to use a spline approach2, the fitted model that will achieve this optimal wigglyness-penalized objective is in fact a type of spline called a Smoothing Spline 🤯, which is similar but not identical to a Regression Spline. So, in this writeup I want to flesh out the details of what exactly a Smoothing Spline is, in more depth than I was able to cover in class.

The “Smoothing Spline Theorem”

The unique function \(\hat{f}\) which is capable of jointly satisfying these constraints (minimizing loss subject to a given “wigglyness” penalty \(\lambda\)) is in fact a special kind of spline that we covered in class: a Natural Cubic Spline. Whereas a Cubic Spline in general estimates separate Cubic Polynomial Regressions for each “piece” of the domain of \(X\) listed above, a Natural Cubic Spline is one which reduces the “roughness” of \(\hat{f}\) at the two extreme ends of \(X\)’s domain (the points close to \(a\) and the points close to \(b\)) by enforcing that a simpler linear (rather than cubic) fit should be estimated for the first piece \([a,\xi_1]\) and the last piece \([\xi_k, b]\) of \(X\)’s domain.

The “Smoothing Spline Theorem”3 says that if we construct a Natural Spline with knots located at each unique \(x\) value in a given dataset, this Natural Spline is guaranteed to minimize our original criterion in Equation 1!

TipThe (Optional) Proof

You may have noticed that I usually try not to include proofs in my slides4, since they can be scary and 99.9% of the time they don’t add enough to the learning to make that scariness worth it.

In this case though, if you’re feeling courageous about using the extra time you might have during Spring Break to “deep dive” ISLP content, this may be a perfect first proof for you!5 It has a structure that is used over and over again in statistics, since it’s how we justify calling something “the” optimal solution to some given problem.

In this case, we’d like to prove that Smoothing Splines are the optimal solution to the minimization problem in Equation 1. To accomplish this, we’ll carry out a proof by contradiction:

  • We’ll start by assuming that we are wrong, and that there is some other function \(\tilde{f}\) that achieves an even better loss-wigglyness balance,
  • But then we will derive a logical contradiction from this assumption,
  • Which will thus indicate that our original assumption must have been incorrect, and that there is in fact no other function \(\tilde{f}\) that achieves a better balance than \(\hat{f}\)

So, let’s start with the assumption, that there is another function \(\tilde{f}\) that achieves a lower penalized loss value than the Smoothing Spline \(\hat{f}\). Then, since the MSE term can always be minimized to zero by perfectly fitting the \(N\) datapoints, the “advantage” of \(\tilde{f}\) over \(\hat{f}\) must come from the second “wigglyness” term. Define \(h(x) = \hat{f}(x) - \tilde{f}(x)\). Then, our assumption that \(\tilde{f}(x)\) has a lower wigglyness than \(\hat{f}(x)\) means

\[ \begin{aligned} &\int_{a}^{b} \hat{f}''(x)^2 dx = \int_{a}^{b}\left(\tilde{f}''(x) + h''(x)\right)^2 dx \\ &= \int_{a}^{b}\tilde{f}''(x)^2 + \int_{a}^{b}h''(x)^2 + 2\int_{a}^{b}\tilde{f}''(x)h''(x)dx \end{aligned} \]

That last term, with the 2 in front of it, is what we care about here
